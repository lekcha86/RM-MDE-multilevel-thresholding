## Reviewer 1

**R1.1 — *Provide the complete derivation showing that Otsu, Kapur and MCET share the segment-additive form.***

We agree and will add an appendix. In brief, with segment masses and moments as in Section 2:

- **Otsu.** The between-class variance is σ_B² = Σ_k ω_k μ_k² − μ_T², with ω_k = m₀/N and μ_k = m₁/m₀. Hence ω_k μ_k² = m₁²/(N m₀). Only the sum depends on **t**, which gives S_Otsu = m₁²/m₀ up to the positive factor 1/N and the constant μ_T².
- **Kapur.** The class entropy is H_k = −Σ_{i∈k}(p_i/ω_k) ln(p_i/ω_k) = −A/ω + ln ω, which is S_Kapur exactly.
- **MCET.** The Li–Lee cross entropy is D(**t**) = Σ_i i h(i) ln i − Σ_k m₁ ln(m₁/m₀). The first term is independent of **t**, so minimizing D is equivalent to maximizing Σ_k m₁ ln(m₁/m₀).

**R1.2 — *Does the O(1) move remain constant for all positions and criteria?***

The cost of evaluating one candidate move is O(1) for every position and every criterion, because it needs four lookups into precomputed cumulative arrays (C₀, C₁ for Otsu and MCET; additionally Σp and Σp ln p for Kapur). What is *not* constant is the cost of a whole sweep. A sweep probes up to L candidates per threshold, i.e. O(nL), and it may be repeated. We will state this distinction explicitly, since the current wording ("O(1) local search") is imprecise. See also R2.4.

**R1.3 — *Handling of zero-probability bins.***

In the implementation, the term p ln p is set to 0 where p = 0, so empty bins contribute nothing to the Kapur cumulative array. A segment with zero mass (m₀ ≤ 0, or ω ≤ 0 for Kapur) is assigned score 0, and for MCET a segment with m₁ ≤ 0 is also assigned 0. This is the standard limit of the respective terms, but it means an empty class is neither penalized nor excluded. The convention is stated in the manuscript (Section 2). We did not count how often empty classes occur in intermediate (repaired) solutions during the search. For all reported reference optima, no optimal segmentation contained an empty class; this will be verified and stated.

**R1.4 — *Circularity in the reference optimum for n > 3.***

We agree that this was a genuine weakness. We have now removed it. For all 120 instances (3 criteria × 8 images × n = 4–8) we computed the certified global optimum with an exact DP over the cumulative-moment representation, independently of RM-MDE's local search. **The previously used reference values coincide with the DP optima in all 120 instances (relative difference 0 to machine precision).** The reported success rates are therefore unchanged, but they now rest on a certified reference. The DP is described in Section 4 of the revised manuscript; a runtime comparison against RM-MDE is still to be added (see R2.2). We also verified that no certified optimum contains an empty class.

Exhaustive search was used only for n ≤ 3 (validation, Table 1). For n = 4–8 the reference is the exact DP optimum described above. The revised text will say so and will no longer describe a "best of methods plus multi-start" reference.

**R1.6 — *Why only eight images?***

We accept that eight images limit generalization over image content. The study, however, targets *optimizer reliability on a fixed objective*, which depends only on the histogram, and the eight histograms were chosen to span unimodal, bimodal and strongly multimodal shapes. We will (i) state this scope, (ii) not interpret the 120 configurations as independent image samples (see R1.11 and R2.6), and (iii) we have not extended the benchmark to a larger set of images, which we state as a limitation, so that the per-image breakdown is not driven by a few histograms. We will list the histogram characteristics (number of modes, empty-bin count) in a supplementary table.

**R1.9 — *Were the competitors tuned?***

We thank the reviewer for raising the issue of baseline tuning. The baselines were not tuned for this problem: their parameters came from the cited sources or common defaults, and the revised Methods section states the provenance of each setting. We have now added a prespecified robustness check of the baseline parameters (manuscript Section 5.8; full grid in the Supplementary Material, `Supplementary_baseline_sensitivity.md`). Using the same 720-instance protocol and the DP-certified optima, we evaluated 16 baseline configurations or checks: nine DE (F, C_r) combinations, three PSO variants, and two equal-evaluation population-size settings for each of DE and PSO. The main DE setting (F = 0.5, C_r = 0.9) achieved the highest success rate among the nine DE combinations (19.0%), the others ranging from 0 to 11.5%. PSO success varied with the topology and coefficients, from 0.6% to 12.1% at equal generations, and the population-size checks did not materially improve either baseline (0 to 1.0% for DE, 0 to 9.9% for PSO). The relative performance of the PSO variants depended on the comparison budget: the global-best topology reached 12.1% against 0.6% for the ring topology at equal generations, but under the seed-matched time budget it reached 42.1% against 48.3% for the ring topology, so we do not conclude that either topology is better in general. Under that time budget the main DE setting reached 32.6% and the PSO variants 42.1–48.3%, against 90.1% for RM-MDE; RM-MDE had the strictly smaller gap in at least 574 of the 720 paired runs for each equal-generation configuration and the strictly larger gap in at most 15. These results indicate that, within the tested parameter ranges, the qualitative advantage of RM-MDE is not sensitive to the baseline parameterization. We state the limits in the manuscript: the grids are small, the same eight images are used, the ring-and-inertia PSO variant and the population-size checks were not run at matched time, and a conclusion about better-tuned baselines beyond these ranges is not established. For synergetic DE and for ISSA, the two baselines whose settings did differ from their sources, see R2.3 and the SDE sensitivity analysis in the same section: the setting reported by Ali et al. is now the baseline (17.6% against 0.6% for the first-version configuration at n = 6–8, and 12.8% for the same configuration with a fixed scale factor), and ISSA was also run at G = 30.

**R1.10 — *Effect sizes and confidence intervals.***

We agree and have revised the statistical analysis (manuscript Section 5.6; `stats_image_level.py`; raw output in `stats_image_level.json`, `stats_image_level_master.csv`, `stats_image_level_perimage.csv`). The protocol was fixed before any test was run. The unit of inference is the image (N = 8); the primary outcome is the mean relative gap to the DP-certified optimum, averaged within each image over the ten seeds and over n = 6, 7, 8, separately for each criterion (the mean rather than the median because the per-seed median gap of the stronger methods is exactly zero); the secondary outcome is the success rate (gap < 10⁻⁸), which is also pooled over criteria. For every comparison we now report the Hodges–Lehmann estimate of the paired difference with its exact distribution-free confidence interval (96.1% for N = 8, the nearest level the exact signed-rank distribution allows to 95%), the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation, the number of images favouring each method, the raw and the Holm-adjusted p-value (Holm applied within the named families: baselines, multistart, donor ablation), and, for success rates, confidence intervals from a bootstrap that resamples the eight images (not binomial intervals that would treat runs as independent). The very small p-values of the former analysis are no longer emphasised; with N = 8 the smallest attainable two-sided p is 0.0078, and a family of six tests cannot be adjusted below 0.047.

**The revised analysis shows that the evidence for differences in objective gap is criterion dependent rather than uniformly significant.** Against PSO and DE under the matched budget, RM-MDE has the smaller mean gap in all eight images for Kapur and for cross entropy (Holm-adjusted p = 0.047), but for Otsu it is better in seven of eight images without reaching significance after correction (PSO 0.15, DE 0.078). Against multistart local search the gap difference is significant only for cross entropy (all eight images, p = 0.047); for Otsu and Kapur it is not (Holm p = 0.59), although RM-MDE has the smaller gap in six to seven images. The success rate pooled over criteria is higher for RM-MDE in all eight images against every competitor (Holm p = 0.016; Hodges–Lehmann differences of 42, 59, 68 and 51 percentage points against PSO, DE and the capped and converged multistart). The Otsu gap result is explained mainly by Cameraman: there RM-MDE has a larger mean gap (2.2×10⁻⁵) than PSO (5.4×10⁻⁶) or DE (1.5×10⁻⁵) because 4 of its 30 runs ended with gaps above 10⁻⁴ (up to 2.9×10⁻⁴) while its median gap is zero; the baselines usually stop at a small non-zero gap. Frequency of success and magnitude of failure are different aspects of reliability, and the revised text reports both. We did not replace the primary outcome by a median or log-transformed gap after seeing this result.

**R1.11 — *Dependence among repeated runs and among instances from the same image.***

## Reviewer 2

**R2.1 — *The reference values do not establish global optimality.***

We agree with the criticism as it applied to the submitted text, and have corrected it (see R1.4). Using an exact DP we certified the global optimum of all 120 instances; **the submitted reference values equal the certified optima in every case**, so Tables 2 and 5 are unaffected numerically (the former Table 4 has been replaced; see R2.3) but are now recomputed against, and reported relative to, independently certified optima. In the revision we will:

- report the reference objective values and the tolerance (relative gap < 10⁻⁸, which is also the numerical resolution at which distinct threshold vectors become distinguishable in double precision);
- additionally report *exact-vector recovery* (identical threshold vector to the DP solution), which is stricter than the objective tolerance;
- distinguish three notions explicitly: an exact coordinate update, convergence to a coordinate-wise optimum, and attainment of the global optimum. RM-MDE's local search guarantees only the first two; the third is established empirically against the DP.

**R2.2 — *Position the contribution against exact and hybrid methods; include an exact solver.***

We fully agree that Eq. (1) admits an O((n+1)L²) dynamic program, and we should not have implied that exhaustive enumeration was the only exact route. We have implemented it and it now supplies the certified reference optima (R2.1); a runtime comparison with RM-MDE is still to be added. We will also (a) cite the polynomial multithresholding literature, the exact Otsu solver compared against metaheuristics, and hybrid DE for thresholding; (b) reposition the paper accordingly; and (c) separate the novelty clearly from the established cumulative-moment representation and the general practice of memetic search.

We wish to be candid about the consequence. For a single 256-level histogram with a fixed segment-additive criterion, an exact DP is practical, and the revised paper will say that the practical justification for RM-MDE therefore cannot be "the only way to reach the optimum". The honest remaining arguments are (i) criterion-agnosticism without requiring the segment-additive/DP structure (RM-MDE needs only constant-time *delta* evaluation of a move, whereas DP needs a full segment-score table), (ii) scaling to larger L (e.g. 16-bit images) where O(L²) tables become expensive, and (iii) the diagnostic finding that common metaheuristics fail to reach optima. We have now quantified the runtime trade-off (Experiment R1; `runtime_scaling.py`, results in `runtime_A_L256.json` and `runtime_B_scaling.json`; AMD Ryzen 9 5900HX, 31 GB RAM, Python 3.13, NumPy 2.2, single thread). At L = 256 the DP, including construction of the score table, takes 1.4–2.5 ms per instance, whereas RM-MDE takes a median of about 0.13–0.34 s (three images, n = 2–8, 10 seeds), so the DP is roughly two orders of magnitude faster. In a scaling test on synthetic histograms (Cameraman requantized to L = 64–2048 levels, n = 6, 5 seeds, 2 at L = 2048) the DP remained faster at every L and criterion, by a factor of about 4 to 9 at L = 2048; we observed no crossover. We state two caveats in the manuscript: the DP is vectorized NumPy while RM-MDE is a pure-Python loop, and L > 2048 was not tested (the O(L²) table then limits memory). **We therefore concede that RM-MDE has no runtime advantage over the exact DP for these criteria**, and the revised manuscript says so. The case for RM-MDE rests on the diagnostic reliability assessment of the metaheuristics and on its requirement of only a constant-time move evaluation; where a full segment-score table is available, the DP is the preferable tool. The runtime comparison is reported numerically in the manuscript's Scope and limitations subsection; we do not add a figure.

**R2.3 — *The computational comparison is incomplete.***

We agree that equal NP and G do not give equal budgets. We have replaced the earlier PSO-only comparison (the former Table 4) with a new experiment (`time_matched.py`; raw results in `time_matched_DE_PSO.json`/`.csv` and `time_trace_DE_PSO_RMMDE.csv`) covering **PSO and DE, all three criteria, all eight images, n = 6, 7, 8 and ten shared seeds (720 paired runs per method)**, all measured against the DP-certified optimum (tolerance 10⁻⁸) on one machine and one thread. The budget is a *seed-matched wall-clock budget*: for each image, criterion, n and seed, the elapsed time of the RM-MDE run with that seed (median 0.23 s, range 0.10–0.53 s) is granted to PSO and DE, which are also run to two and three times that time. Because the budget is set by RM-MDE's own run it varies between seeds, and it is applied at generation granularity (a baseline's result at budget B is its best value after the first generation completing at or after B, which favours the baselines).

The results are as follows. At the matched (1×) budget the success rate is 90.1% for RM-MDE, 48.3% for PSO and 32.6% for DE. With two and three times the budget PSO reaches 57.6% and 59.7%, whereas DE remains at 32.6% (it stagnates early; only 3 of its 720 runs improve after the 1× budget). The mean relative objective gap at 1× is 1.7×10⁻⁵ for RM-MDE, 3.7×10⁻⁴ for PSO and 2.9×10⁻⁴ for DE (the median gap of RM-MDE is zero), and the gap-versus-time curves (new Figure, manuscript Section 5.4) show RM-MDE descending faster than both baselines for each criterion. Paired by seed, RM-MDE is strictly better than PSO in 359 runs, tied in 335 and worse in 26, and against DE 473, 224 and 23. **The advantage is not universal**: it is widest at n = 8 and narrowest against PSO at n = 6 with triple the budget (PSO 76–86% against 92–96%), and PSO exceeds RM-MDE on two of the 72 image–criterion–n instances (Coffee/MCET/n=6: 100% against 90%; Cat/Kapur/n=7: 90% against 80%). We have worded the conclusions accordingly and restricted them to the tested settings (untuned baselines, eight images). We have not used confidence intervals computed from these 80-run cells as inferential evidence, since the runs share images; the dependence-aware analysis is described under R1.11.

Concerning ISSA, an implementation audit against the published paper (equations (1)–(11), Table 2 and the experimental settings, checked on the PDF) identified an error in our implementation: in the discoverer and entrant branches a rejected candidate position was not restored although the previous fitness was retained, so a stored position could differ from the position that attains its stored fitness (the returned thresholds did not attain the reported fitness in 720 of 720 audit runs). We corrected it (greedy acceptance, with an invariant check that the returned thresholds attain the stored fitness), kept the earlier version in the code as `ISSA_legacy` for traceability, and replaced all ISSA-specific results. **All previously reported ISSA numbers are superseded.** With the corrected version, the mean success over the 120 instances changes from 0.0% to 3.5%, the Friedman mean rank from 5.00 to 4.86 (in the regenerated summary, together with the SDE correction), the PSNR of ISSA on the cross-entropy criterion at five thresholds from 17.0 dB to 29.67 dB (the earlier collapse was caused by the error, not by the algorithm), and the main-table success rates at n = 2, 3, 4 change by up to a few tens of points (for example Otsu n = 3: 42% to 60%). The qualitative pattern is unchanged: the adapted ISSA is at zero from five thresholds on at G = 60, and also at G = 30. Because the published algorithm targets two-dimensional maximum-entropy thresholding with a threshold pair, we describe ours as a one-dimensional adaptation that follows its update equations (cosine inertia weight, Lévy flight, greedy acceptance) on the ordered-threshold vector with the common repair and objective; we have removed the phrases "faithful reimplementation" and "normalized search space" (the code did not normalize the search space). Two minor differences from the paper's wording remain and do not matter: the paper's equation (10) multiplies by δ_u although u is already N(0, δ_u²), which we implemented in the usual single-scaling form (720 runs: success 0.00% against 0.14% with the literal form), and its vigilant-sparrow branch for f_i = f_g is applied by us to f_i ≤ f_best. The conclusions are confined to this adapted implementation and not to the published algorithm.

**R2.4 — *Numerical feasibility and cost specification.***

All points are accepted:

- *Zero-mass classes and logs at zero:* specified as in R1.3.
- *Collision repair:* round, clip to [2, L], sort; then a forward pass sets t_k ← t_{k−1}+1 where t_k ≤ t_{k−1}, and a backward pass resolves any residual collisions at the upper boundary.
- *Local-search stopping and tie rules:* we scan every admissible position between the neighbours and move to the position of greatest gain; a move is accepted only if the gain exceeds 10⁻⁹ (so ties are resolved in favour of the current position, then the lowest index among equal gains). **The sweep is capped at 4 per invocation in the implementation; the manuscript's statement "the sweep repeats until no single-threshold move improves" was therefore inaccurate and will be corrected.** The 4-sweep cap does not guarantee a coordinate-wise optimum on every call.
- *Index mapping and the intensity offset in MCET:* internally, bins are indexed i = 1…256 and bin i holds gray value i−1; thresholds are bin indices (the gray-value thresholds quoted in Table 1 equal the bin index minus one). We thank the reviewer for pointing out that the intensity offset is not generally innocuous for minimum cross entropy. We therefore performed an independent sensitivity analysis rather than assuming offset invariance (`mcet_offset_validation.py`; results in `mcet_offset_validation.json/.csv`, `exact_optima_mcet_gray0.json`, `mcet_offset_rmmde_comparison.json`). For all eight images and n = 2–8 (56 instances) we recomputed the exact optimum with the physical coordinate x = i−1 (gray values 0–255) using a separate exact DP; the x = i results reproduce the earlier certified values exactly. **The certified optimum changed in 19 of the 56 instances (34%; 13 of 40 with n ≥ 4)**, so MCET is not invariant to a unit offset and the optimal threshold vector is sensitive to it (seven of eight images are affected; the shift is usually one level, but reaches 34 levels in one Astronaut case). The objective loss from using the optimum of one convention under the other was, however, small: at most 8.7×10⁻⁵ (x = i thresholds evaluated under x = i−1) and 6.3×10⁻⁵ (opposite direction), relative to the optimal value of the evaluating convention. We do not compare the raw objective values of the two conventions, which are on different coordinates. Numerically, no certified optimum contains an empty class (m₀ = 0). Under x = i−1 a class consisting only of gray level 0 has positive mass but zero first moment; five such candidate classes occur among the eight histograms and one of the 56 optima contains one. We score them with the limiting value S = 0 (m₁ ln(m₁/m₀) → 0 as m₁ → 0), and the same rule is used in the DP and in RM-MDE. RM-MDE (10 seeds) reached a mean success rate of 95.9% under x = i and 93.9% under x = i−1 against the respective exact optima. We **retain x = i** because it is the mathematical coordinate i = 1,…,L used in the multilevel MCET formulation we follow (Yin, 2007), while the images themselves use the physical levels 0–255; the revised manuscript states this explicitly, reports the sensitivity above (new Section 5.2), and notes that the cross-entropy results are specific to this convention. (Otsu is invariant to the offset, since the sum changes only by a constant, and Kapur does not use intensity values.) Because the main experiments use x = i, they did not need to be repeated.
- *Sweeps in the cost bound:* the cost expression will be revised to O(G·NP·(n + p_ls·s·nL)), where s ≤ 4 is the (observed) number of sweeps; and the O(n log n) sort in the repair will be included. We will report measured counts of probes and sweeps alongside runtime.

**R2.5 — *The ablation does not support all explanations.***

We agree that the former Table 6 supported only the statement that adding the exact local search improves DE, and that it could not separate the contributions of the donor, of selective refinement and of the population. We have replaced it with three controlled experiments (`ablation.py`, `multistart.py`; raw data in `ablation_A/B_*` and `multistart_C_*` in the released results), all on minimum cross entropy (x = i) for the donor and p_ls experiments and on all three criteria for the multistart comparison, with eight images, n = 6, 7, 8, ten seeds, the same objective, repair, initialization and four-sweep cap, and the DP-certified reference (tolerance 10⁻⁸). The rand/1 variants with and without local search reproduce the main-study code exactly for the same seed (checked run by run), and no run beat the certified optimum. The new manuscript Section 5.7 reports them.

(i) *Donor isolation (240 runs per variant).* We crossed three donors (rand/1, best/1 and the tournament-best donor of synergetic DE, the last isolating the donor only, not the full SDE, with the scale factor fixed at the common F = 0.5) with the absence or presence of local search (p_ls = 0.15). At the fixed 60-generation budget local search raises the success rate of every donor from 5–26% to 81–91%. Once local search is present, rand/1 (90.8%) and the tournament-best donor (89.6%) are close, whereas best/1 is lower (80.8%), with the difference arising at larger n (n = 8: 86.2%, 86.2% and 65.0%; at n = 6 rand/1 and best/1 are equal). Paired by seed, rand/1 + LS beats best/1 + LS in 37 runs, ties in 190 and loses in 13, and against the tournament-best donor 20, 205 and 15; at the image level the difference to the tournament-best donor is not significant (smaller mean gap in 3 of 8 images, Holm p = 0.84). **A previous version of this analysis, in which the synergetic donor carried a randomized scale factor (not in the source paper), had shown it clearly behind rand/1 (78.8%); that gap came from the scale-factor setting and has been withdrawn.** The setting F = C_r = 0.25 reported by Ali et al. gives 53.8% with local search under our 60-generation budget (fewer accepted trials: a mean of 44 local-search calls against 129), which we report as a sensitivity result. The variants with local search take about 2–4 times longer (0.20–0.33 s against 0.07–0.08 s), so this effect is measured at equal generations, not equal time; best/1 and the tournament-best donor were run at the p_ls chosen for rand/1 and were not tuned.

(ii) *Refinement probability (p_ls = 0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00; rand/1).* Success rises from 17.5% (p_ls = 0) to 78.3% (0.05) and 90.8% (0.15), and then shows diminishing returns (92.1%, 92.5% and 90.8% at 0.25, 0.50 and 1.00; paired comparisons against 0.15 are near balanced). Runtime grows roughly in proportion to p_ls, from 0.25 s at 0.15 to 1.27 s at 1.00. At the fixed 60-generation budget, refining every accepted trial therefore gave no higher success rate than refining 15% of them, at about five times the cost. We do not claim that selective refinement is better in general (a fully refining variant with fewer generations was not tested), and p_ls = 0.15 was fixed beforehand, so this is a retrospective sensitivity check. The observed local-search work (calls, sweeps, moves and probes per run, and calls per accepted trial) is recorded in the released data, which answers the request in R2.4.

(iii) *Population versus repeated random starts (720 paired runs, three criteria, seed-matched wall-clock budget).* RM-MDE was compared with multistart exact local search (uniform random feasible starts refined by the same local search until the RM-MDE runtime of that seed had elapsed; one variant with the same four-sweep cap, one supplementary variant run to convergence). RM-MDE reached 90.1%, against 22.5% and 38.1% for the two baselines; paired by seed it was strictly better in 539 and 430 runs, tied in 155 and 267 and worse in 26 and 23. At the level of the 72 image–criterion–n instances the capped baseline exceeded RM-MDE in 3 and the converged baseline in 7. Among successful runs the median time to the first success was 0.33 of the budget for RM-MDE, 0.35 for the capped and 0.19 for the converged multistart, so we do not claim that RM-MDE reaches the optimum faster, only that it does so far more often within the budget. These results are consistent with the interpretation that the population provides more effective starting points for refinement than uniform random restarts; they do not show that population search beats every multistart strategy (for instance one with a better initialization), nor which part of the population mechanism is responsible.

(iv) *Diversity.* We measured the mean distance of the population from its centroid per generation. It falls from 85.1 at initialization to 22.5 (rand/1, no LS), 5.7 (rand/1 + LS), 0.7 (best/1) and 9.4 (tournament-best donor with fixed F, no LS) by generation 20, and to 2.3, 0.3, 0.0 and 0.5 by generation 60. best/1 loses diversity fastest, consistent with its lower success at larger n, but the rand/1 population with local search also collapses to near zero, the tournament-best donor is less diverse than rand/1 at generation 20 yet has the higher success without local search (26.2% against 17.5%), and the F = C_r = 0.25 setting keeps the most diversity (9.3 at generation 60 with local search) yet has the lowest success of the local-search variants (53.8%). Diversity alone does not account for the differences. **We have therefore withdrawn the statement that rand/1 preserves diversity and the population-collapse explanation of the swarm operators' failure**; the revised text presents the diversity measurements as observations and states that the causal explanation is not established.

Overall, the revised manuscript says that the results *indicate* that exact local refinement accounts for the large gain in reliability at a fixed generation budget, that the donor adds a smaller contribution once refinement is present, and that under the tested wall-clock budgets the population outperforms uniform random restarts. We have removed the word "decisive" and make no factorial or variance-decomposition claim.
